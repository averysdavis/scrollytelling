Understanding the poem

Here are three different translations to try to get at the meaning.

The first translation is my own and prioritizes the meaning a Chinese reader would get while sacrificing the experience of an English reader.

The second is by John Cayley and Grace Boynton and is quite an experience for the English reader but adds meaning not in the original Chinese.

The third, by Julia C. Lin, retains the simple conciseness of the original but again adds its own meaning.

And here is a word-by-word translation, to see the meaning as a Chinese reader would. The square brackets [] indicate a grammatical term.

The three translations translate 繁fán three ways: “vast,” “a maze of,” and “infinite.” 繁fán means many, dense, and intricate, and no English word contains all of these meanings.

Chinese verbs don’t conjugate to carry tense, so tenses, unlike in English, are optional. Tenses are often indicated with additional, optional grammatical markers. 着zhe is one of them and marks an ongoing action.

What is a one-syllable marker in Chinese is either dropped in translation or translated as “continuously,” adding length the original did not have.

The dash leaves the line open, but only one translation includes it.

The entire second line is a noun phrase.

The 的de is merely a grammatical character indicating that deep blue (深蓝) is an adjective modifying the noun expanse (太空).

The term 太tài空kōng would be understood by a modern Chinese reader as “outer space,” but an early 20th-century reader would have understood it as “expanse,” with a plainer connotation than the English word has.

Chinese does not differentiate between singular and plural nouns, so the line could be translated as “deep blue expanse” or “deep blue expanses.”

The parataxis in Chinese is very concise and plain while leaving the relation unstated.

The first translation recreates the simplicity but loses some of the connection to the rest of the poem.

The second and third translations set out to capture the feeling of the original by spelling the relation out, but in turn they lose the simplicity of an isolated phrase.

Chinese grammar creates an ambiguity that English cannot. The original Chinese line has no subject.

This shifts focus from whoever could or could not hear the dialogues to the creators of the dialogues themselves.

The closest English can get to this is using the passive voice, as in the first translation, to shift the focus.

In the third line, the term 何hé曾céng is a classical rhetorical marker. It contrasts with the modern vernacular of the previous lines.

听tīng得de见jiàn doesn’t ask if the dialogues were heard but whether hearing them is possible.

对duì语yǔ is an unusual word choice. The more common, conversational term for dialogue or conversation would be 对duì话huà.

对duì语yǔ gives a more literary feel, again making this line feel more formal and older than the rest of the poem.

Additionally, 对duì means face-to-face or reciprocal, suggesting the dialogues are mutual.

它tā们men means “they” in English, but in Chinese it indicates that the “they” are not human. While pronounced the same, Chinese uses 他tā们men for human “theys” and 它tā们men for animals or inanimate “theys.”

None of the translations recreates the contrasting literariness of this line, and the second and third translations add subjects not mentioned in the original.

Lines four and five have parallel three-character phrases with a noun plus a version of “inside” (中/里). Silence is about sound and glimmer about light, so the poem moves from hearing to seeing.

It answers the rhetorical question of line three: the stars’ conversations are not heard; instead, they are in silence and light.

沉chén默mò is an interesting term. 沉chén by itself can mean to sink or submerge (into water), to indulge or to give in to, dark or gloomy, or long and prolonged.

默mò by itself can mean to be silent, or secretly.

When combined, the phrase 沉chén默mò evokes a feeling of weighted, consuming silence, not just the lack of sound.

On its own, 微wēi can mean tiny, faint, waning, or obscure.

光guāng means light as a substance, not brightness.

When combined, 微wēi光guāng means a faint light that feels like it’s barely there.

中zhōng and 里lǐ both mean “in” or “within,” but 中zhōng marks an abstract, enclosed space (as silence is a condition you are inside of) while 里lǐ marks a more concrete area (as faint light is a substance you are surrounded by).

Bing Xin’s choice of 中zhōng for 沉chén默mò and 里lǐ for 微wēi光guāng is a deliberate asymmetry.

The English words “in” and “within” only hint at this, and the phonetic contrast is lost.

Again, Bing Xin uses 它tā们men, specifying that the stars are not people. This is in contrast to the personification that immediately follows.

For a second time, we see the character 深shēn, meaning deep. In this instance, it is doubled, making it heavier and slower than its first use while describing the depth of the praise.

互hù相xiāng, meaning mutually, reminds readers again that the stars’ conversations are reciprocal.

颂sòng赞zàn, like the word choice in line three, again shifts the poem’s register away from plain and simple. 颂sòng赞zàn, meaning to praise or extol, is liturgical. The stars’ praises are devotional worship.

了le indicates that the worship is complete and the action is no longer ongoing.

Here we get another answer to the question in line three: the stars are not heard, but they have spoken, and their speech is deep, mutual worship.

All the English translations have a lot of active language.

The original Chinese, however, has a much more passive feel. Chinese has a tendency to drop the subjects of sentences, meaning that what happens isn’t caused by anything or anyone in particular.

Only my translation and Lin’s keep Bing Xin’s indents.

Even then, they differ: every Chinese character fills the same square, so an indent is exactly one character. English letters vary in width.

Line length is impossible to match. In Chinese, one character is one syllable, so the lines run five, five, nine, three, three, and ten, on the page and aloud.

Vast Stars (1) by Bing Xin

Vast stars twinkle continuously—
　Deep blue space,
　Could their words ever be heard?
Within silence,
　In the glimmer,
　　They have deeply, mutually chanted praises.

A Maze of Stars (1) by Bing Xin

A maze of stars glistens
in the deep azure vault of sky.
When before have I overheard their whispers?
In the profound silence,
in their own faint light,
this deep acknowledgment of one another.

Infinite Stars (1) by Bing Xin

Infinite stars sparkle
　In the deep blues of sky
　Does anyone ever hear them talk?
In the depths of silence
　Within this faint light
　　They brightly sing one another’s praise.
